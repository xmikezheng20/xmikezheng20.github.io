During a recent visit home, I had a comprehensive physical exam that included a brain MRI. I later sat in a clinic to discuss the results briefly with a senior doctor and a few student doctors. The results were normal, so the conversation was casual and friendly. At some point, one of the student doctors asked me what I did. I told him I was a PhD student in neuroscience.

He seemed interested. “What disease do you study?” he asked.

I hesitated for a second, because the answer I usually give when people ask me what I work on did not fit the shape of this question. I stumbled, but nonetheless told him that I study the neural circuits of vocalizations in rodents. He looked a little confused, perhaps unsure what to do with my answer. The senior doctor, perhaps sensing the small awkwardness, explained that my work was basic research. The conversation moved on.

This short exchange stuck in my mind. We were all, in some sense, working near the nervous system, but our approaches were strikingly different. In the hospital setting, it was natural to assume that neuroscience was organized around diseases. In my world, it was equally natural for neuroscience to mean questions about brain and behavior that may have no immediate clinical relevance at all.

I came to graduate school broadly interested in how the brain functions. Around that time, systems neuroscience was changing quickly. New tools made it possible to simultaneously record from many neurons in freely moving animals. Deep-learning-based methods were making it easier to quantify behavior. There was also a renewed interest in neuroethology — in asking not only how the brain solves tasks designed by experimenters to model cognitive processes, but also how animals generate behaviors that matter in their own lives.

This confluence led me to join the Banerjee Lab and study the neural circuits of vocal communication in Alston’s singing mouse, a rodent native to Central America known for its loud, stereotyped vocalizations during social interactions. When I joined, we were a new lab working with a nontraditional animal model, so we decided to spend a lot of time trying to understand the behavior itself. This was partly to learn what these vocalizations might mean in the animal’s natural life, and partly to build a foundation for future neural manipulation and recording experiments. We watched the mice, handled them, and read papers on zoology and animal behavior. In the process, we made many small observations, formed tentative predictions, and adjusted our experiments based on what the animals actually did. Through many iterations of this process, we started to develop a practical behavioral intuition: when they vocalized, what social contexts mattered, and what they seemed to like or dislike.

Over time, this growing familiarity with the animals also changed the science in our lab. We found that singing mice not only produce their namesake songs, but also robustly produce ultrasonic vocalizations, or USVs, a conserved vocalization common to many rodents. We also improved our social behavioral paradigms, so that a dataset that once took roughly sixty hours to collect could later be collected in around six hours. The coexistence of ancestral and novel vocalizations within a single species, sometimes even within a single behavioral session in a single animal, made it possible to directly compare the underlying neural circuits. In this sense, careful observation helped us move from characterizing a behavior to using singing mice as a model for studying how new behaviors evolve.

I saw a similar form of observation and care turn into practical knowledge during one of my lab rotations. In that lab, researchers trained mice to perform a continuous sensorimotor task. On paper, the task could be described cleanly. In practice, training an animal required a great deal of judgment. The experimenter had to watch how each mouse approached the task, when it was confused, when it was engaged, and how to adjust the training gradually. This kind of intuition can deeply shape how a researcher thinks about behavior and the brain.

These experiences shaped my understanding of what PhD training actually involves. Of course, we are supposed to become subject experts on our thesis projects. But this training also teaches something equally important: a way of seeing. We learn to watch carefully, to notice patterns, to form expectations, and to revise them through experiment. We learn that small details can matter. We learn that a surprising observation is not yet a hypothesis, but it may become the beginning of one.

Later, during the same trip home, I spent time in a neurology ward because of an elderly family member. I was there as family, not as a scientist, and certainly not as a clinician. In the quiet periods between rounds and exams, I found myself watching the doctors, nurses, patients, and families come and go in the hallway.

I had encountered neurological and psychiatric disorders mostly from textbooks and introductions to research talks, where each disease is often summarized by a few characteristic features: anhedonia in depression, learning and memory deficits in Alzheimer’s disease, motor deficits in Parkinson’s disease, and so on. Researchers make animal models of these disorders, develop phenotyping methods based on some of these defining features, and use the models to understand mechanisms and develop interventions. But in the ward, what struck me was the density and variety of the phenomena. Disease did not appear as a clean behavioral variable. A patient who had walked gingerly with the help of a stick could, a few minutes later in another context, walk with large and balanced strides. A patient with Parkinsonian tremor could also be unable to stay asleep for more than a couple of hours. I did not know how to interpret these moments, and I do not want to overread them. But that was partly what made them striking to me: they were phenomena I would not have known to think about without being there.

I left with the feeling that many clinically rich observations remain largely invisible to basic neuroscience trainees like me. Clinicians see these phenomena every day, but they are often busy caring for patients. Basic scientists have tools for building models and testing mechanisms, but many of us rarely encounter the clinical realities that might enrich or challenge our models. When such observations do enter basic neuroscience, they often do so through chance conversations, unusual career paths, or individual physician-scientists who can move between worlds.

There is nothing new about saying that basic neuroscience and clinical medicine are separated. The gap between bench and bedside is a familiar concern. Physician-scientist training exists partly to bridge it, and many important discoveries have come from people who can think across clinical and basic sciences. But my experience made me wonder whether there is another, lighter-weight bridge from the basic research side: not one that turns basic scientists into clinicians, but one that gives them some direct exposure to clinical phenomena while their scientific questions are still taking shape.

Young basic scientists are often shaped early. Many develop their academic interests through undergraduate or post-baccalaureate research, before they even enter a PhD program. Once in graduate school, they rotate through a few labs, join one, and then spend years within a particular model system, set of methods, and local scientific culture. This narrowing is necessary to do deep work in a field. But a side effect is that, even as trainees develop general research skills, their scientific imagination can become local to the systems and questions immediately around them.

For basic neuroscience trainees, some clinical exposure could potentially broaden that imagination. This is not to say every project needs a translational angle. Basic science should remain free to study mechanisms that have no immediate medical application. But clinical settings contain forms of behavior and variability that are difficult to appreciate from papers alone. Seeing them may shape what a young scientist pays attention to, what models they use, what tasks they design, and what phenomena they study.

The current system does not make this easy. A PhD student’s time is precious, and advisors understandably want students to make progress on their thesis work. Clinicians are busy, and hosting a basic science trainee may not bring them any obvious tangible benefit. Clinical settings also involve privacy, credentialing, and other logistical barriers. Nevertheless, many basic neuroscience trainees might benefit from seeing clinical neuroscience more closely, and clinicians might have observations that could be interesting to basic researchers. There is often no simple forum where these interactions are expected, supported, or rewarded.

I think a short clinical exposure program for neuroscience PhD students and early postdocs could provide a scaffold for this kind of exposure. It could be as simple as a few weeks or a summer spent shadowing in neurology, psychiatry, rehabilitation medicine, sleep clinics, or related clinical research settings. The goal would be modest: to give developing basic scientists structured contact with clinical phenomena as they find their scientific questions and form their scientific taste. This would not by itself solve a disease or explain the brain. But it may be a way of engineering serendipity: creating a setting where young scientists are allowed, even briefly, to see what clinical medicine sees every day.

I still think about that brief conversation in the clinic. The distance between me and the student doctor need not be as large as it seemed to me at the time. Although we study the brain in different states, in different individuals, and sometimes in different species, we are all students of the brain. I do not think every basic scientist needs to become translational, or that every clinical observation needs to become a research program. But I do think young neuroscientists may carry a richer sense of the nervous system they study if they have the chance to see it from both sides.
